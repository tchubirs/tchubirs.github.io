Why in the browser: a bank statement or a client invoice should not be uploaded to a stranger's server to become a spreadsheet. Reads: text-based PDFs. A scan with no text layer needs OCR, which this does not do.

Why this one is different

The file stays on your computer

Every other converter asks you to upload the PDF to their server. Tablecut does the reading here, in this tab, with your browser's own PDF engine. Nothing is sent anywhere — you can turn off your internet after the page loads and it still works.

That is the whole point. A bank statement, a client invoice, a payroll sheet: those are exactly the documents you are not allowed to hand to a company you have never heard of — and exactly the ones people end up retyping by hand.

It shows you what it read

The table on screen is what you are about to paste. Check it before you paste it, fix a row, drop the lines above the header.

A converter that quietly drops a digit is worse than no converter at all, because you find out three weeks later when a total does not match. Tablecut would rather show you a messy table than a wrong clean one.

Who ends up using it

  • Bookkeepers and small accounting offices — statements and invoices that arrive as PDFs from a dozen different banks and suppliers, every month.
  • People who handle someone else's documents — where uploading is not a preference but a rule you would be breaking.
  • Anyone told "just retype it" — reports, price lists, listings that only ever come as PDF.

If your own system produced the data, you do not need this. Export a CSV and go. This exists for documents that arrive from outside.

Honest limits

  • Scanned pages do not work. If the PDF is a photograph of a document there is no text to read, and Tablecut tells you so instead of inventing rows.
  • Hard layouts need a nudge. Two sliders — row grouping and column gap — and a click to say where the table starts. That is the whole adjustment.
  • It reads tables, it does not understand them. It will not know which column is VAT.
